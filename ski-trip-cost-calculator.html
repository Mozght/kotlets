How to estimate a ski trip without mixing up days and nights

Ski trips have a habit of hiding their real price in different units. The pass is often per person per day, the chalet is per night for the group, lessons are a package, and the train is a one-off. If you add those figures as if they were the same kind of number, you will either under-count the week or scare yourself with a total that is too high. This ski trip cost calculator keeps the units explicit and then gives you a group total and a cost per person.

Begin with people, ski days and nights. They are not the same. A Saturday-to-Saturday week is seven nights. If you travel on the first and last day, you may only ski six days. Lift passes and rental “per day” rates in this form use ski days. Lodging uses nights. Food uses calendar days, which default to nights + 1 — because you still eat on the travel days.

Passes, rentals and lessons

For lift passes, prefer an official price for your dates and age band over a remembered number from a previous season. Many resorts sell a trip or 6-day pass that is cheaper than six times a one-day ticket. If you already have that package price, choose “per person for the trip”. If you only have a daily rate, choose “per person per ski day”. The same logic applies to rentals: a week package is a trip rate; a shop that charges each day is a daily rate. If you bring your own skis or board, leave rentals at zero and put a tune, bag or insurance fee in “other”.

Lessons are often the line that applies to only part of the group. If two of four people are in ski school, do not multiply a lesson price by four. Either enter a group total for the lesson bill, or a per-person amount that already reflects only the people taking lessons. Child and adult prices are usually different, so add them before you type the figure.

Lodging, food, travel and a buffer

Chalet and apartment quotes are typically for the whole place per night, or for the week as a single invoice — both of those modes are in the form. Hotel rooms are sometimes priced per person per night. Food in a self-catered apartment is lower than in a resort restaurant town; be honest about which kind of trip you are actually taking. Travel should include the journey to the resort and the last transfer, not only the headline flight or train.

A 10% buffer covers tourist tax, parking, lockers and the day the weather pushes you into a café. Raise it if you have not booked the pass yet. The results card shows the group total, the cost per person, and a per-ski-day figure for the whole group so you can compare resorts on the same basis. Frequently asked questions

What should I include in a ski trip cost?

The core lines are lift passes, equipment rental or your own gear costs, lessons, lodging, food, and travel to the resort. Add insurance, parking or a resort tourist tax in “other”. This calculator keeps lift and rental “per day” rates on ski days, and lodging on nights, so a travel day without skiing is not charged a pass.

How do I estimate lift passes and rentals?

Use official prices for your dates and ages, not last year’s brochure. A resort often has a trip pass that is cheaper than multiplying a one-day ticket. Rentals can be a trip package or a per-day rate — pick the unit on the form. If you already own skis or a board, leave rentals at zero and put any tune or bag fee in “other”.

Are ski days the same as nights?

Usually not. A Saturday-to-Saturday week is seven nights and often six ski days if you travel on the first and last day. Food still follows calendar days (nights + 1 by default). Lift and rental daily rates in this calculator use the ski-day figure.

How do I get a cost per person?

Enter the group rates you were quoted, and the calculator totals everything and divides by the number of people. If one person is taking lessons and another is not, put lessons in as a per-person amount only for the people who are taking them — or use a group total for the lesson bill you were given.
